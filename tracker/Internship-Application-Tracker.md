> [!important] The standing rule needs a third clause: WHEN is the standing measured?
> The rule as it has been applied — *"any sentence naming a class year is a hard stop"* — has now over-rejected at least three live New York seats. The missing distinction is **the moment the clause is evaluated against.**
>
> **Evaluated at APPLICATION time, against a fixed class year → genuinely disqualifying.** Morgan Stanley Early Insights: *"second-year undergraduate students graduating between December 2028 and June 2029."* He is a credit junior **now**, so he fails **now**. MLT: *"College sophomore graduating in Fall 2028, Spring 2029…"* — same shape, same failure. Lincoln International: *"Graduating in Winter 2028 or Spring 2029 (undergraduate students in their sophomore year of study)"* — same.
>
> **Evaluated at INTERNSHIP time, relative to graduation → describes him exactly.** Jefferies: *"be completing the junior year"* **and** *"graduating from December 2028 to June 2029."* For a Spring 2029 graduate, AY 2027–28 **is** the junior year, so Summer 2028 is the summer after it. The two clauses agree; they are the firm's shorthand for one cohort. Greenhill/Mizuho's *"penultimate year"* and LionTree's *"rising seniors"* are the same construction — and **this file already accepted exactly this reasoning for UBS Tomorrow's Talent** (*"second-to-last year of undergraduate study"*).
>
> **The Blackstone case is neither.** *"students in their junior year"* paired with a **near-term** graduation — there the band itself excludes him. The class-year words were never the disqualifier; the graduation timing was.
>
> **Practical rule going forward:** read the standing clause **and** the band together. If they describe the same cohort and the band is his, he is in. If the standing clause contradicts the band for him *today*, he is out. He reports credit standing and graduation date truthfully in every case and never self-describes as a sophomore.
